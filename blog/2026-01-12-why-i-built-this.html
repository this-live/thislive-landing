Why I built This.Live

By Bryce Murad · January 12, 2026

Laid off on a Tuesday, building by Thursday. The honest beginning.

January 6

I got laid off on January 6, 2026. That is the honest beginning of this company, and I would rather start with it than with a mission statement.

I had spent five years as a network architect building enterprise infrastructure for large organizations across financial services, healthcare, and government. I was good at the work. I knew how to translate messy requirements into network designs, cost models, diagrams, RFP responses, and systems that could survive contact with real customers. I liked the customers more than the politics, which is a common thing to say and also true.

Then the job ended, and I had a decision to make. I could spend the next stretch trying to get back into the same lane, with the same kind of employer, doing the same kind of work for another few years. Or I could use the moment to build the thing I kept circling around anyway.

I started building on January 8. Two days. There was no sabbatical, no period of reflection, no vision board. There was a terminal window and a list of things I had been wanting to exist.

The quiet weeks

This matters for how you read everything after it: the public launch of This.Live happened in March, about eight weeks after the layoff. The real work started in January. Those eight weeks were not a company yet. They were me, a couple of machines, and a growing pile of infrastructure that did not have a name on it.

I am saying this plainly because founder origin stories get polished into something unrecognizable. Mine is not a story about vision. It is a story about momentum. I had been building agents and automations on the side for a while, and the layoff removed the excuse to keep them on the side.

By the time anyone saw a landing page, there was already a lead generation product taking shape, a routing layer being sketched, and a small fleet of machines in my house doing real work. The launch in March was a reveal, not a beginning.

Concretely, the first thing I built was Beacon. The idea came from the sales work I knew best: find local businesses with weak or missing websites, and reach them with something more useful than a cold pitch. Within the first two weeks it could search businesses by geography and niche, flag which ones had no web presence, manage them through a pipeline, and draft the outreach. It even placed calls. I am not claiming it was polished. I am claiming it worked, and that building it in January, before the company had a name in public, taught me the speed this stack was capable of.

What I am actually building

The short version: This.Live is an AI-native software company run by one person and a fleet of agents on hardware I own. The slightly longer version is that I am building the products and the infrastructure at the same time, because I do not think you can do the first well without owning the second.

The products you will read about in this archive: Beacon, Maestro, Mnemos, and a line of games and tools that came later. The infrastructure is less photogenic and more important. Memory that respects project boundaries. Routing that treats model choice as an engineering decision instead of a default. Agents that leave receipts so I can verify what they did instead of trusting what they said.

I am not trying to sell a fantasy about replacing people. I am trying to build leverage without losing judgment. Every system I write about here has a human accountable for it, and that human is me.

The human reason

I like building. I like coaching. I like taking something messy and turning it into a system that works.

Swimming taught me that perfect practice is not romantic. It is repetitive, occasionally boring, and brutally honest. You do not get to argue with the clock. Network architecture taught me that production systems do not care how clever the diagram looked. They either route traffic or they do not.

This.Live is me applying those lessons to software and agents. Hard work works. Truth matters. You help people when you can. You stay composed when things break, and things will break.

What this blog is

One post per week, written by me, about what I actually worked on. Not thought leadership. Not content marketing. A public build log from someone trying to do something that has not quite been done: run a real software company as one person, with machines doing most of the labor and a human doing all of the deciding.

Some weeks will be about products. Some will be about the infrastructure under them. Some will be about things that broke and what I learned putting them back together. I will not claim numbers I cannot pull from the systems themselves, and I will tell you when I have not solved something yet.

Eight weeks from this post, I launched. I will write about that when we get there.
