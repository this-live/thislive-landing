Beacon taught me to respect the messy middle

By Bryce Murad · April 6, 2026

What public beta actually means

Public does not mean proven

Beacon is public. I want to tell you exactly what that means, because the word "public" does a lot of dishonest work in launch posts.

It means the product exists, the workflows run, and real people outside my house can touch it. It does not mean the business claims around it are proven. A route can be live. A workflow can be useful. A product can be genuinely functional and still be pre-revenue, unproven at scale, and one bad week away from teaching me something uncomfortable. All of those things are true of Beacon at once.

For anyone new to it: Beacon is a lead engine for local businesses. It searches businesses by geography and niche, flags the ones with weak or missing websites, manages them through a pipeline, and drafts outreach that is supposed to be more useful than a cold pitch. It grew out of the sales work I knew best before this company, and it was the first thing I built after the layoff, in the quiet weeks before anyone had heard of This.Live.

The messy middle is where most products actually live. You have enough built to learn from reality and not enough evidence to talk like the scoreboard is settled. The temptation in that position is to borrow confidence from the future. Write the post as if the outcome already happened. I have felt that temptation every time I write about Beacon, and this post is me declining it in public.

The useful lesson

What Beacon has already given me is not revenue. It is a set of questions I could not have asked without building it, and they are better questions than the ones I started with.

How do you find businesses without turning outreach into spam? Anyone can scrape a directory. The hard part is the filter: which business actually benefits from hearing from me, and which one just got annoyed. Getting that wrong does not just waste my time. It spends the reputation of a company that cannot afford to be known as noise.

How do you show a prospect something useful without inventing facts? A generated demo is only worth sending if every claim in it is true about their business. The moment the pipeline fills in a plausible detail that is not real, the whole thing becomes the exact kind of AI slop I built the content gates to reject. The standard has to be the same for outbound as it is for the blog.

How do you track attribution honestly? If a business responds, I need to know which step earned it, or I cannot improve anything. And underneath all of these: how do you make local-service marketing useful to the person receiving it instead of tolerable at best. I do not have final answers to any of these. I have working answers that get less wrong each iteration, and I consider that the actual product progress.

Compliance is a feature

One thing the beta made non-negotiable: the boring rules are not overhead. Outreach that touches real businesses has to respect consent, identification, and opt-outs, and it has to do that structurally, not as a promise in a settings page. A lead engine that cuts those corners is not aggressive. It is broken, and it is borrowing against every future customer.

I would rather Beacon grow slower inside clean rules than faster outside them. That is easy to say in a blog post, so let me make it concrete: the constraint has already killed approaches that would have produced more leads this month. I do not regret any of those decisions, and I expect to make more of them.

I should also admit that the compliance work is not finished. Rules written in April will meet situations in June that nobody designed for. The point is not that the current rules are perfect. The point is that there is a rulebook at all, that it lives in the pipeline instead of in my intentions, and that when it conflicts with growth, growth loses. Most lead tools get this backwards and then wonder why their domain reputation collapses.

What comes next

The path from here is receipts, in the same sense I use that word for the fleet. Real operators, real outreach, real responses, recorded honestly enough that I can tell the difference between "people replied" and "people replied because the thing was useful." Product loops that make sense for the businesses on the receiving end, not just for my dashboard.

That is slower than making a loud claim. It is also the only version I am willing to build, because a lead engine with a reputation problem is worse than no lead engine.

I will write the revenue post when there is revenue to write about, and I will include the numbers that are embarrassing alongside the ones that are not. Until then, Beacon is what I said it is: public, working, unproven, and teaching me more per week than anything else I have shipped.
