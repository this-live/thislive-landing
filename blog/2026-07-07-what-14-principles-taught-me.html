What 14 principles taught me about operating as one person

By Bryce Murad · July 7, 2026

I had 14 founder principles. I cut them to 9. Here is what the compression taught me.

Editing your own operating system

There is a difference between writing principles and writing principles you actually use. I learned this the hard way, the same way you learn most things about running a solo operation: by accumulating enough good-sounding advice that you could no longer tell which ones were guiding your decisions and which ones were just making the document look impressive.

I had fourteen principles. They were all sensible. They were all things you could put on a whiteboard in a coworking space and get nods from everyone in the room. The problem was that not all of them were actually guiding my decisions. Some of them were just decorations. I was writing principles that sounded good on a document without checking whether they showed up when I was actually building Cortex or deciding what to ship with Forge.

Thirteen to nine.

The compression happened during a cycle of heavy redesign work. The philosophy page was rewritten from portfolio framing to substrate framing. The founder operating principles document was going through its own revision. And as I sat down to edit the principles, the first thing I noticed was that half of them were redundant, and a few of them were just things I had added because they sounded mature and principled.

I went from fourteen to nine. The five that came off were not bad principles. They were just not the ones I was actually following when it mattered. The ones that survived were the ones that showed up in real work. The routing principle that lives in every Maestro decision. The one that governs when Cortex writes to MNEMOS versus when it caches locally. The one that says every agent touches Telegram only through scoped receipts. Those showed up. The others lived on the page and nowhere else.

Where I stand

Along with the compression, I added a section to the philosophy page called "Where I stand." This is not a principle. It is a stance. There is a meaningful difference between the two, and I only understood that difference after wrestling with it for a while.

A principle is a guideline. It tells you how to behave. A stance is a claim you can be wrong about. You can disagree with a stance and make a defensible case. You cannot really be wrong about a guideline, because guidelines are descriptive of your own preferences. A stance forces you to take a position that someone else could push back on, and that pushback is where the real thinking happens.

When you are building alone, there is nobody to push back on your principles. The "Where I stand" section is the closest thing I have found to that. It is a place where I say something that other people might disagree with, and that disagreement is useful. It tells me whether my position is actually thought through or whether it is just a comfortable thing I have been saying to myself.

Portfolio to substrate

The philosophy page rewrite was part of the same cycle. The old page described a person with a collection of projects. One operator, many products. Each one a chapter. It was honest, but it was the wrong kind of honest. It told you what I had built without telling you what I was building toward.

The new page describes a system with a position. The substrate. The local memory that survives every model change, every surface change, every dispatch change. The commit message was simple: "family influences, reframe the existing 21 principles." But the work was not simple. It was not adding content. It was changing the frame. That change of frame altered how someone reads every product page that follows, whether they land on the Cortex page, the Maestro page, or the Surfaces overview.

This is the kind of work that does not show up in a changelog. Nobody merges a PR and says "I changed the philosophy frame from inventory to direction." But it matters. The frame determines what the reader expects to find on the next page. The right frame makes the next page land. The wrong frame makes the reader braced for something else.

The solo operator pushback problem

When you are the only person building and writing, there is no one to challenge your principles. No colleague to say "wait, do you actually follow rule four, or did you just add it because it sounded good?" No manager to ask why a principle that looks great on paper is not showing up in any decision you made last week.

You have to push back on yourself. The distillation process is the pushback. Cutting 14 principles to 9 is a form of self-interrogation. Each cut requires answering a question that is harder than it sounds: did this principle actually guide a decision, or did I just add it because it looked good on a page? Most people never have to answer that question about themselves. In an organization, there are enough voices that some of them will notice the gap between stated principles and actual behavior. When you are one person, the gap is invisible until you force yourself to look at it.

The compression made the gap visible. That is its primary value. Not the resulting nine principles. The fact that fourteen became nine, and that the reduction came from cutting the ones that were not doing work rather than the ones that were uncomfortable to keep.

What I will say in three months

When I look at these nine principles three months from now, I will know which ones actually shaped the next quarter and which ones I already forgot. Some of them will have guided real decisions about Cortex and Maestro. Others will have been useful as a checkpoint and then irrelevant. The honest update I owe you is which of the nine held up and which ones are just ghost principles now, sitting on a document that nobody actually reads.

I am not going to claim that nine is the right number. It might be eight in three months. It might be ten if I realize something important was cut too quickly. What I can say right now is that the process of getting from fourteen to nine was the real work, and that process is available to anyone who runs a solo operation. Edit your own operating system the same way you would edit code: aggressively, honestly, and with the willingness to cut the parts that only look good in isolation.

Thanks for reading. I will share the three-month follow-up when it is ready.
