Fieldhouse and FTAG are two different game lanes

By Bryce Murad · April 13, 2026

Sports games and Future Throwback Arcade Games

The distinction matters

I keep the games work at This.Live in two lanes, and I want to explain why, because blurring them is the fastest way to build the wrong thing twice.

Fieldhouse is the sports-game lane. Underserved sports, arcade feel, mobile-first loops, and a path toward a broader portfolio over time. The bet behind Fieldhouse is that the big sports titles serve the biggest sports and leave everyone else playing games that were never really made for them. A swimmer, a rower, a field hockey player does not have a game that understands their sport. That gap is the lane.

FTAG is Future Throwback Arcade Games. Original spiritual successors to the games that made the browser era and early mobile feel immediate. Not remakes, not clones, not nostalgia wallpaper. New games built on the design instincts that made the old ones impossible to put down.

These two lanes share an engine philosophy and nothing else. A sports game has real-world constraints: the sport itself is the design document, and if the game disagrees with how the sport actually works, the players who love that sport will feel it in the first minute. A throwback arcade game has no such anchor. Its source material is memory, pacing, and feel, which are harder to specify and easier to fake. Different instincts, different failure modes, different definitions of good.

Age of Arrows

The first FTAG game in active development is Age of Arrows. I am not going to describe it feature by feature here, because the interesting decision is not what it is. The interesting decision is what I chose to study before building it.

The goal is not to copy the old games. It is to isolate the part that worked and build forward from that. Readable pressure: you always know what is about to kill you. Upgrades that change how you play, not just how big your numbers are. Simple inputs with real depth underneath. Enough mastery that one more run feels worth it at midnight. Those properties are why those games survived in people's heads for twenty years, and they are cheap to name and expensive to reproduce.

The clearest modern proof that this still works is Balatro. Everyone who talks about Balatro talks about the jokers. I think the jokers are the least important part. The lesson is the way between-run upgrades make you feel your power curve changing. You are not just stronger on run twelve. You are playing a different game than you were on run one, and you got there through choices you understand. That feeling is the entire retention mechanic, and it is the part I am studying hardest for Age of Arrows.

Build with taste

Games punish vague thinking faster than any other software I have built. A SaaS tool can survive a boring week. A game cannot survive a boring minute. If the loop is dull, the player leaves and does not file a bug report explaining why. If the numbers feel fake, the player feels it in their hands before they could articulate it. There is no enterprise contract smoothing over a mediocre core loop.

That is why both lanes run on prototypes, playtests, and honest iteration rather than branding exercises. The brand work is real work, but it is downstream. A beautiful page for a game that is not fun is a liability with good typography.

I should be honest about resourcing, because this is a one-person company and these lanes are not the revenue plan. Fieldhouse and FTAG get built in the margins that the infrastructure and the products leave behind, and I protect that time on purpose. The games sharpen the same skills the rest of the company runs on: loops, feedback, restraint, respect for the person on the other end. They also keep the work fun, which is not a soft benefit. It is load-bearing.

There is a practical upside to the two-lane split that took me a while to see. Because the lanes share nothing but philosophy, each one gets to be honest about its own timeline. Fieldhouse does not have to pretend it will ship this quarter, and Age of Arrows does not have to carry the weight of a whole portfolio narrative. Each lane has its own bar and its own clock. That separation has already saved me from at least one announcement I would have regretted.

Where this goes

Near term, Age of Arrows gets to a playable state before it gets any louder treatment. Fieldhouse stays in design until the first sport earns a prototype, and the first sport will be one I know well enough to be embarrassed by a fake version of it.

I will write the playtest post when there is something worth putting in a stranger's hands, and I will report what they actually did, not what I hoped they would do. If the loop is boring, you will read that here first.
